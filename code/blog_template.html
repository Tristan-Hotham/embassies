Every country keeps a small piece of itself in other people's capitals. I mapped {{data.missions|int}} embassies, consulates and other missions from open data, drew every country's network, and followed the diplomatic web back from today to the clay letters of Amarna.

An embassy is an odd thing when you think about it. A few buildings in a foreign city, flying another country's flag, staffed by people who answer to a government thousands of kilometres away. Put all of them on one map and you get something like a portrait of how states see each other: who bothers to be present, who shares a street, and who is never in the same room.

There is no official list of every embassy in the world, but there is open data. OpenStreetMap volunteers have mapped {{data.osm_features|int}} diplomatic buildings, and Wikidata lists thousands of embassies by who sends them and who receives them. I cleaned, merged and checked the two. The result is {{data.missions|int}} current missions sent by {{data.senders|int}} states: {{data.by_type.embassy|int}} embassies, {{data.by_type.high_commission|int}} high commissions, {{data.by_type.nunciature|int}} papal nunciatures, {{data.by_type.consulate_general|int}} consulates-general, {{data.by_type.honorary|int}} honorary consulates and more.

Count every post, consulates included, and {{networks.posts.0.name|raw}} edges ahead with {{networks.posts.0.n|int}}. I was worried that open data would be patchy, so I checked: for the seven countries whose totals the Lowy Institute published from its 2024 Global Diplomacy Index, this map finds between {{coverage.lowy_min_pct|pc}} and {{coverage.lowy_max_pct|pc}} of Lowy's number. That is far better than I expected from volunteers.

Embassies are expensive to move, and some never follow a new capital. Tanzania's {{moves.tanzania.emb_old|int}} mapped embassies are in Dar es Salaam, not Dodoma; Cote d'Ivoire's {{moves.ivory_coast.emb_old|int}} in Abidjan, not Yamoussoukro; Myanmar's {{moves.myanmar.emb_old|int}} in Yangon, not Naypyidaw. Of {{israel.embassies|int}} embassies to Israel, {{israel.tel_aviv_area|int}} are in the Tel Aviv area and {{israel.jerusalem|int}} in Jerusalem, whose status is disputed. Where a move did work, the consulates stay behind: Lagos keeps {{moves.nigeria.cons_old|int}} consulates while {{moves.nigeria.emb_new|int}} embassies sit in Abuja, and Switzerland splits neatly into {{switzerland.bern|int}} embassies in Bern and {{switzerland.geneva_perm_missions|int}} missions to international organisations in Geneva. {{fewest_hosted.0.name|raw}}, meanwhile, hosts no resident embassy at all.

From Amarna to today

Diplomacy is very old; the embassy that stays is not. Around 1350 BC the pharaoh's court at Amarna exchanged letters, envoys and gifts with the kings of Babylon, Mitanni, Hatti and Assyria. For three thousand years most envoys came, talked and went home. Resident ambassadors appear in Renaissance Italy in the 1440s and 1450s; the Ottoman Empire opened its first permanent embassy, in London, only in {{deep.ottoman_first|raw}}. The interactive timeline plays a small sample of {{deep.events|int}} famous missions before handing over to the Correlates of War data, which record which states were represented in which from 1817 to 2005. A caution: that early sample is a handful of famous cases, not a census. I checked each date against the article I cite for it: {{deep.confirmed|int}} lines are confirmed, {{deep.approximate|int}} approximate and {{deep.not_confirmed|int}} not confirmed, and the map shows which is which.

{{IMG|b_deep.png|Map of Europe, the Middle East and Asia with coloured arcs: purple lines from Egypt to Babylon, Hatti and Cyprus for the Amarna letters, pink arcs from Rome and Constantinople to China and Baghdad, and orange and yellow arcs from European capitals to Constantinople, India, Japan and Siam in later centuries|A sample of famous missions before 1817, coloured from purple (oldest) to yellow.}}

In 1817 the system had {{cow.states_1817|int}} states, and only {{cow.amb_1817|int}} of {{cow.rep_1817|int}} links were at the rank of ambassador, a great power's privilege; the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies hosted almost as many envoys as London. By 2005 there were {{cow.states_2005|int}} states and {{cow.amb_share_2005|pc}} of links were ambassadors. The waves of new states after 1918, 1960 and 1991 stand out, and so do the Cold War's rival networks: in 1965 West Germany was represented in {{cow.frg_1965|int}} states and East Germany in {{cow.gdr_1965|int}}; ten years later, after the two recognised each other, the East had {{cow.gdr_1975|int}}.

How I did this

I took every OpenStreetMap object tagged as a diplomatic office, worked out which state sends it (from its tags, its linked Wikidata item or its name, in English and 22 other languages) and which receives it, and dropped residences, offices of international organisations and things that were not diplomatic at all. I merged duplicates and matched the result with Wikidata's embassies, consulates and representative offices. Two of the Wikidata classes I had downloaded turned out to be schools and television channels, so they went. Records of Taiwanese embassies in states that have since switched recognition were flagged as out of date. For every country I compared its 2026 network with what the Correlates of War data recorded in 2005, which gives a rough coverage check; the network abroad looks complete for {{coverage.abroad_good|int}} of {{coverage.benchmarked|int}} countries. The caveats matter: a dot is a mapped mission, not proof that it is open today; consulates and honorary consulates are mapped very unevenly; and where recognition is contested, the map records what the data say and takes no side.
